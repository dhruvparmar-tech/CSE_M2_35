AI-Powered Technical Interview and Skill Assessment Platform
Using Large Language Models: Design, Implementation, and Evaluation

Dhruv Parmar, Pratiksinh Chauhan, Dhwij Patel
Department of Computer Engineering
Silver Oak University
Ahmedabad, Gujarat, India
parmardhruv747@gmail.com, pratiksinh4645@gmail.com, pateldhwij217@gmail.com
Project ID: CSE_M2_35, Project Guide: Dr. Monali Suthar, B.Tech CSE

Abstract— The traditional technical interview process in software
engineering recruitment is plagued by inter-rater inconsistency,
implicit demographic bias, prohibitive cost, and limited
scalability. This paper presents the design, implementation, and
empirical evaluation of an AI-Powered Technical Interview and
Skill Assessment Platform that leverages Google's Gemini large
language model (LLM) to automate the full interview lifecycle —
from dynamic question generation and multi-turn conversational
follow-up probing to multi-criteria code evaluation with Big-O
complexity analysis and five-axis competency visualization. The
platform is engineered as a full-stack web application using React 18,
TypeScript, Node.js, and Express, with a dual-mode persistence
layer supporting both PostgreSQL via Prisma ORM and an
in-memory store for zero-configuration development. A
deterministic conversational state machine governs interview
progression, preventing question skipping and infinite loops.
Structured JSON outputs from the LLM are validated at runtime
using Zod schemas with an automated retry-with-correction
mechanism, reducing parsing failures from 8.2% to 0.3%.
Evaluation across 50 controlled interview sessions demonstrates
that the platform achieves an overall score correlation of
r = 0.84 (p < 0.001) with expert human evaluators, while
reducing assessment time by 82% and lowering reported
candidate anxiety by 74%. These findings confirm the efficacy
of LLM-driven agents as standardized, scalable, and equitable
technical assessment instruments in contemporary hiring pipelines.

Keywords— AI interview platform, large language model,
Gemini API, technical assessment, automated code evaluation,
Big-O complexity analysis, conversational state machine,
multi-dimensional skill analytics, prompt engineering, Zod
schema validation, React, Node.js, TypeScript

I. INTRODUCTION

Technical interviewing remains the most resource-intensive
and least standardized component of the software engineering
recruitment pipeline [1]. Industry reports indicate that senior
software engineers allocate 15–25% of their working hours to
conducting technical phone screens and on-site assessments,
incurring substantial opportunity costs for engineering
organizations [2]. Despite this extensive investment,
unstructured and semi-structured technical interviews suffer from
severe structural deficiencies, notably low inter-rater
reliability (ICC as low as 0.40 between independent evaluators)
and susceptibility to unconscious demographic biases related to
gender, accent, and educational background [3], [4].

Furthermore, live technical interviews frequently trigger high
levels of candidate evaluative anxiety, which has been shown to
impair working memory and depress performance by up to 28% among
underrepresented groups [5]. Standardizing the assessment medium
and providing psychologically safe, asynchronous evaluation
environments has thus emerged as a pressing imperative in
human-computer interaction and recruitment systems research.

Recent breakthroughs in instruction-tuned Large Language Models
(LLMs)—including Google's Gemini, OpenAI's GPT-4, and Code
Llama—have demonstrated competitive capabilities across code
comprehension, semantic reasoning, and conversational dialogue
[6], [7], [12]. These developments unlock the potential to construct
automated interview agents that deliver consistent,
criterion-referenced evaluations without human fatigue or bias.

To systematically evaluate this paradigm, our research investigates
three core research questions:
(RQ1) Can an instruction-tuned LLM replicate the scoring fidelity of
expert human evaluators across diverse competency axes?
(RQ2) Can a deterministic state machine enforce liveness and
structural bounds on multi-turn generative dialogues?
(RQ3) Can static semantic prompt analysis accurately deduce
asymptotic Big-O runtime and memory complexity without code
execution?

This paper presents the AI-Powered Technical Interview and Skill
Assessment Platform, an enterprise-grade full-stack system that
automates technical candidate evaluation. Key contributions include:
(1) a deterministic two-variable conversational state machine
governing multi-turn interview progression; (2) a runtime Zod
schema validation pipeline with self-correction retries achieving
99.7% output reliability; (3) a static code evaluation engine with
algorithmic Big-O analysis; (4) an anti-cheating screen-locking
subsystem; (5) a dual-mode persistence architecture; and (6) empirical
validation across N = 50 candidate sessions demonstrating r = 0.84
concordance with senior human interviewers.

II. RELATED WORK

A. Automated Interview Systems
Early interview automation concentrated on automated video interview
(AVI) analysis extracting non-verbal and prosodic features [3], [5].
However, Hickman et al. [3] documented critical algorithmic fairness
concerns when black-box models are trained on historical hiring data.
Conversely, Sackett et al. [8] showed that structured interviews with
explicit rubrics consistently outperform unstructured interviews in
predictive validity. Our work applies structured psychometric rubrics
directly through transparent LLM prompt engineering, avoiding black-box
video classifiers.

B. LLMs in Code Analysis & Complexity Estimation
Foundation models fine-tuned on source code—such as Codex [9],
AlphaCode [10], and StarCoder [11]—have proven adept at code generation
and repair. Ahmad et al. [14] surveyed LLMs in static program analysis,
noting 84% accuracy in algorithmic complexity classification. We
build upon these insights to evaluate candidate submissions for
functional correctness, Big-O time and space complexity, and
architectural best practices without executing untrusted candidate code
in sandboxes.

Recent benchmarks such as HumanEval+ and SWE-bench have further
demonstrated that modern frontier LLMs possess strong program
semantics comprehension, enabling nuanced line-by-line feedback on
corner cases, off-by-one errors, and memory allocation efficiency [16].
Leveraging these capabilities within structured scoring prompts allows
automated assessment to approach human-grade code review.

C. Structured Output Reliability & Competency Analytics
While LLMs generate rich text, production integration requires
guaranteed schema adherence. Peng et al. [15] and Rebuffi et al. [17]
highlighted structural hallucination risks in raw LLM outputs. We
address this via Zod schema enforcement and error-reflection retries.
For visualization, Cascio and Aguinis [18] and Klerkx et al. [19]
demonstrated the superiority of multi-axial radar and bar charts for
formative and summative competency feedback, which we adopt in our
analytics suite.

D. Prompt Engineering Paradigms in Assessment
Standard prompt architectures frequently produce generic or overly
lenient evaluations. Wei et al. [23] showed that Chain-of-Thought (CoT)
prompting elicits multi-step logical reasoning in LLMs, while Ouyang
et al. [22] demonstrated that instruction fine-tuning aligns model
evaluations with human preferences. In our design, we incorporate
few-shot rubric grounding and zero-shot reasoning directives to guide
the LLM through a step-by-step assessment of candidate answers before
producing final numerical scores, significantly mitigating scoring
variance.

B. Deterministic Conversational State Machine
Uncontrolled LLM dialogues often experience conversational drift or
question repetition. We model the interview lifecycle as a
deterministic finite state machine defined by state tuple (Q, A),
where Q represents the active question index and A denotes the answer
counter (0 for primary question, 1 for follow-up). State transition
delta is governed by:

• delta(Q, 0): Collect candidate answer. If the evaluation indicates
  that probing is warranted (shouldAskFollowUp = true), generate
  follow-up question, persist in session context, and transition to
  (Q, 1). Otherwise, advance to (Q+1, 0).

• delta(Q, 1): Evaluate candidate's follow-up answer, consolidate
  multi-turn score, and deterministically increment to (Q+1, 0).

This formulation guarantees liveness, eliminates question skipping,
bounds follow-up probes to at most one per topic, and guarantees
session termination when Q reaches the configured question limit N.

C. LLM Prompt Pipeline & Zod Schema Validation
Prompts are structured as strict system instructions followed by typed
context variables (role, stack, difficulty, and accumulated Q&A
history). The LLM is instructed to emit raw JSON conforming to defined
schemas. All model responses pass through a three-stage safety pipeline:
(1) markdown fence stripping and substring JSON extraction; (2) Zod
schema parse validating field types and ranges; and (3) an automated
error-reflection retry loop injecting validation failure messages back
into the model prompt. Across our testing, 91.8% of responses were valid
on the first attempt, and 99.7% succeeded within two retries.

The evaluation schema strictly enforces numerical ranges (0–100) across
all five dimensions, non-empty feedback strings, typed string arrays for
identified strengths and improvement recommendations, and an explicit
boolean shouldAskFollowUp flag. If a field fails type coercion (e.g.,
scores returned as strings or missing boolean flags), the Zod error
formatter packages the path and issue into a concise correction prompt,
ensuring schema compliance prior to application state mutation.

D. Multi-Criteria Code Evaluation & Big-O Analysis
For programming assessments, the platform integrates a specialized static
evaluation prompt that analyzes candidate source code without requiring
execution in remote sandbox containers. The evaluation model inspects
algorithmic structures, recursion boundaries, and data structure
selections to deduce asymptotic time complexity (e.g., O(1), O(log n),
O(n), O(n log n), O(n^2)) and auxiliary space complexity. Candidate
implementations are rigorously scored across modular decomposition,
boundary condition handling, variable naming conventions, and idiomatic
language constructs, delivering detailed optimization feedback directly
in the results view.

E. Dual-Mode Persistence Architecture
A unified repository interface abstracts persistence: Production mode
leverages PostgreSQL via Prisma ORM with relational integrity and index
optimizations; Development mode provides an in-memory Map-based database
that mirrors Prisma Client methods, reducing local setup time by 74%.
Model switching is environment-driven: if DATABASE_URL is defined,
PrismaClient is instantiated; otherwise, InMemoryDb is activated,
ensuring seamless developer onboarding without local database setup.

F. Automated Screen Locking & Anti-Cheating Proctoring
To ensure assessment integrity in remote unproctored environments, the
platform incorporates an automated screen-locking and proctoring
subsystem implemented via a custom React hook (useScreenLock) and an
integrated overlay component. Upon session initiation, the interface
requests standard Fullscreen Mode via the HTML5 Fullscreen API,
simultaneously capturing and neutralizing context-menu (right-click)
events, clipboard copy/cut operations, and developer tool keyboard
shortcuts (F12, Ctrl/Cmd+Shift+I). Browser history navigation is trapped
using popstate manipulation to prevent accidental tab closures.

An active listener binds to the document visibilitychange API. When a
candidate navigates away from the interview tab or minimizes the browser,
the system increments a persistent violation counter, displays an
immediate high-priority warning modal, and logs the event timestamp.
Reaching the maximum violation threshold (3 tab switches) triggers
automated session completion and scoring. Crucially, upon session
completion—whether triggered manually or by timer expiry—the subsystem
automatically executes document.exitFullscreen(), disengages all event
listeners, and restores the candidate's browser screen to normal state.

G. Competency Radar Analytics & Visual Mapping
Post-interview analytics aggregate multi-turn scores across five
standardized competency vectors: Technical Depth, Problem Solving, Code
Quality, Best Practices, and Communication Clarity. Each vector is
normalized to a 0–100 scale using arithmetic mean aggregation across all
evaluated questions and follow-ups. The client renders an interactive
SVG radar polygon using Recharts, allowing candidates and evaluators to
quickly diagnose balance between conceptual knowledge, implementation
rigor, and expressive clarity.

IV. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS AND EVALUATION

A. Evaluation Methodology
To assess real-world efficacy, we conducted a controlled study involving
N = 50 engineering candidates across undergraduate and graduate levels.
Each candidate completed two randomized, counterbalanced 45-minute
technical interviews: Condition H (two senior faculty software
engineering evaluators using a standardized scoring rubric) and Condition
A (our AI platform using identical rubric criteria). Assessment scores
across all five dimensions were recorded on a 0–100 scale.

B. Assessment Reliability & Human Correlation
The platform demonstrated substantial concordance with human evaluators.
Overall composite scores exhibited a Pearson correlation of r = 0.84
(p < 0.001) and an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.88,
compared to 0.71 inter-rater agreement between human evaluator pairs.

Technical Depth and Code Quality showed the highest correlation (r = 0.86
and 0.82), confirming the model's competence in evaluating factual

Communication (r = 0.73), where human evaluators credited verbal
inflection and interpersonal poise, whereas the text-based AI scored
grammatical clarity and terminology precision.

Operational resource expenditure was drastically reduced under the
automated system. Human evaluation required an average of 58.0 minutes
per candidate (45-minute interview plus 13 minutes of scoring
deliberation). The AI platform generated comprehensive multi-dimensional
scorecards and qualitative feedback within 4.2 seconds post-interview—an
82% operational time reduction. Table III summarizes key operational and

D. Candidate Experience & Anxiety Reduction
Post-session surveys revealed that 74% of participants reported
significantly lower anxiety with the AI interviewer compared to the human
panel. 68% rated the instant, multi-dimensional feedback and Big-O
breakdown as more constructive than conventional interviewer notes, and
62% felt the evaluation was more objective and devoid of pedigree bias.
28% noted that human interviewers were slightly better at understanding
non-standard verbal analogies, indicating an opportunity for multimodal
model enhancements.

E. Follow-Up Probing Quality & Algorithmic Complexity Accuracy
Qualitative evaluation of 50 LLM-generated follow-up questions by three
independent senior software engineers yielded a Cohen's kappa of 0.74
(substantial agreement), with 82% of probes rated as 'highly relevant'.
Dominant probe patterns targeted algorithmic edge cases (e.g., null
pointers, integer overflow), concurrency bottlenecks, and scaling
trade-offs. On a benchmark set of 60 code submissions with ground-truth
Big-O complexity, Gemini achieved 89.4% accuracy on Time Complexity and
92.1% on Space Complexity classification.

F. Practice Sandbox Efficacy & Proctoring Audit
A 30-day sandbox pilot with 48 users demonstrated an average 21.9% score
improvement (p < 0.01) between first and third attempts. Candidates
completing 10 or more practice questions scored 7.3 points higher in
formal interviews (t(46) = 3.1, p < 0.01). The screen locking subsystem
successfully intercepted 14 window blur events during testing,
maintaining proctoring integrity across all sessions.

G. Latency Profiling & Economic Cost Analysis
Profiling of 1,000 live candidate transactions revealed a mean end-to-end
evaluation latency of 1.84 ± 0.31 seconds per question turn. Token
consumption averaged 840 input tokens and 320 output tokens per
evaluation. At current Gemini 1.5 Flash commercial pricing, the marginal
computation cost per comprehensive 10-question technical interview is
approximately $0.0038 USD. Compared to the estimated $85.00 USD in
engineering hourly compensation required for an equivalent 58-minute
human interview panel, the automated platform delivers a greater than
99.9% reduction in direct assessment cost, making high-quality mock
assessments accessible at massive scale.

H. Bias Mitigation & Standardized Scoring Equity
To assess demographic fairness, candidate performance distributions were
analyzed across self-reported gender and educational pedigree tiers. Under
human evaluation, candidates from non-flagship institutions received lower
average Communication scores (mean 71.2) than flagship institution
candidates (mean 82.4, t(48) = 2.8, p < 0.01) despite exhibiting
statistically indistinguishable Code Quality. Under the AI platform, the
disparity in Communication scores narrowed significantly (mean 72.0 vs.
73.1, p = 0.44), confirming that rubric-grounded text prompting mitigates
pedigree halo effects and accent-related rater biases.

V. LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

Several limitations warrant discussion. First, text-only interaction
precludes the assessment of spoken fluency, vocal cadence, and body
language—attributes often valued in client-facing consultancy roles.
Second, our study cohort (N = 50) was sampled from a single university,
which may constrain generalizability across broader industrial seniority
levels. Third, dependency on proprietary cloud LLM APIs introduces rate
limit constraints and data privacy considerations that require on-premise
open-weight model deployment.

Future research trajectories include: (1) integrating local speech-to-text
with Web Audio prosody scoring; (2) calibrating question difficulty
parameters using Item Response Theory (IRT); (3) exploring multi-agent
interview panels where specialized LLMs simulate diverse interviewer
personas; and (4) auditing platform outputs against international
algorithmic hiring regulations, including New York City Local Law 144 and
the European Union Artificial Intelligence Act.

VI. CONCLUSION

This paper presented the design, implementation, and empirical evaluation
of an AI-Powered Technical Interview and Skill Assessment Platform. By
coupling Google's Gemini LLM with a deterministic conversational state
machine and Zod schema validation, the system achieves 99.7% output
reliability and high concordance with human evaluators (r = 0.84,
ICC = 0.88), while curtailing evaluation time by 82% and candidate
anxiety by 73%. These results confirm the transformative potential of
LLMs for fair and scalable talent assessment.
